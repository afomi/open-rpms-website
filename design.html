People first

A health program runs on work people do every day: registering a patient, booking a visit, seeing the patient, billing for the care. OpenRPMS starts with that work, not with the software. We describe who does it, what they need to get done, and how the work flows from one person to the next. RPMS and its interfaces are how that work is supported today.

The model lives at ux.openrpms.org. Everything in it uses synthetic demo data.

From people to screens

  1. 1. People

    The roles that do the work: the front desk clerk, the scheduler, the medical assistant, the nurse, the provider, the billing technician, the patient, and more. Each is written up as a persona: the role, the work it does, and what it needs.

    Personas

  2. 2. Needs

    What each person needs to get done, written plainly as a short story. For example: "As a registration supervisor, I want to change a patient's legal name on proof, keeping the old name and the document that justified it, so that the chart follows the patient and the history can be audited." Each story has scenarios that say exactly what done looks like.

    Use cases

  3. 3. Processes

    How the work flows, drawn as a journey: a patient is registered, an appointment is booked, a patient is roomed, a visit is billed. Each step shows who does it and what happens next.

    Journeys

  4. 4. Screens

    Each step is shown on the screen where it happens today. That makes the current experience visible, so a change can be judged by what it does for the person doing the work.

    ux.openrpms.org

Where RPMS fits

A need comes first, and there can be more than one way to meet it. The desktop EHR, the web registration and scheduling screens, a newer web interface, and the terminal are all ways RPMS supports the same work. We are mapping, scenario by scenario, which interfaces meet each need today.

RPMS itself, its database, its packages and its broker calls, is the foundation underneath. It matters, and it is documented elsewhere on this site, but it is not where the design starts. The design starts with the person and the work.

Why work this way

  • The work outlasts any one system, so it is worth describing on its own terms.
  • A need written plainly can be checked: either the person can get it done, or they cannot yet.
  • Changes are judged by what they do for the people doing the work, not by what changed in the software.
  • Clinics, program staff and developers can read the same model and talk about the same thing.

Try it, and tell us

Walk a journey on the demo, signed in as one of its personas. If a step does not match how the work is really done where you are, that is exactly what we want to hear: open an issue.
